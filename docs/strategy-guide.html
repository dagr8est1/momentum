Momentum Investing Strategy

A strategy that rotates money into whichever stocks have recently been rising fastest, backtested with backtrader and packaged as an installable, config-driven Python tool. This doc explains exactly what the strategy does and why, every formula it uses, how it performed over a 28-year survivorship-bias-free S&P 500 backtest, and how the codebase is put together — written to be followable without a finance background.

Terms used in this guide, in plain English

  • Benchmark — the "did nothing clever" comparison. Here it's the S&P 500 (via the SPY ETF): what you'd have earned just buying and holding the market.
  • Backtest — running a strategy against historical prices to see what would have happened, without risking real money.
  • CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) — the one steady annual return that would have turned your starting money into your ending money. The standard way to compare returns over different time spans.
  • Volatility — how much a price bounces around day to day. Higher volatility = a bumpier ride, not necessarily a worse destination.
  • Sharpe ratio — return earned per unit of bumpiness. Two strategies can end up with the same return, but the one with the higher Sharpe ratio got there more smoothly. A Sharpe of 1.0 is solid; under 0 means you'd have done better in cash.
  • Drawdown — the biggest drop from a peak to the following low point before recovering.
  • Rebalancing — periodically adjusting which stocks are held and how much money sits in each.
  • Turnover — how much trading a strategy does, as "how many times a year the whole portfolio gets replaced."
  • Risk parity / inverse-volatility sizing — instead of splitting money evenly, give shakier stocks a smaller slice and steadier stocks a bigger one.
  • Overfitting — tuning a strategy's settings until it looks great on past data, in a way that says nothing about the future — like memorizing last year's exam answers instead of learning the subject.

01
Foundations

The core idea

Rotate into a small book of trending winners, and go to cash when the market itself is trending down. Three ideas from the momentum literature, combined — nothing invented from scratch.

Cross-sectional

Rank stocks against each other every period. Only the strongest relative performers (top 10 by default) are held — the classic Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) momentum effect.

Time-series

Each holding must also be trending up in absolute terms (price above its own 200-day average). Relative and absolute trend must agree — Antonacci's "dual momentum."

Regime-aware

A market-level trend filter can flatten the entire book to cash. The strategy doesn't try to stock-pick its way through a bear market.

Implements: the overall shape of MomentumStrategy.next() in strategy.py.

Foundations

The universe

What's eligible to be ranked in the first place — and why getting this right matters more than any formula on the later slides.

Three interchangeable sources, set by universe.source:

SourceWhat it resolves toSurvivorship bias
staticAn inline list in the config — configs/default.yaml ships ~140 tickers spanning mega-caps, sector SPDRs, factor/thematic ETFs, international/EM, and bonds/commodities, so momentum can rotate into whatever asset class is working.Whatever you put in it
index:sp500Scrapes today's S&P 500 constituent table from Wikipedia at run time.Yes — today's list applied to the past
index:sp500_point_in_timeReconstructs the index's actual membership month by month, from a stored table of monthly constituent snapshots (1996 → 2026). Each rebalance can only pick from the names genuinely in the index on that date.No
Why the point-in-time source exists Backtesting momentum against today's S&P 500 hands the strategy a roster picked with hindsight. Names that joined the index recently — precisely because they went up a lot — are retroactively made available to buy years before they qualified, while companies that were in the index and then failed out of it silently disappear from the test. For a strategy whose entire job is buying recent winners, that is not a small distortion. The point-in-time source removes it: on any given rebalance date the candidate list is the membership that actually existed then, including the names that later got acquired, delisted, or dropped. All results in §2 use this source.

Price history for every ticker that has ever been a member is loaded up front (including long-dead names), and each stock only becomes scoreable once it has enough of its own real trading history to fill every lookback window.

03
Step 1 · Every bar, before anything else

Regime filter — is the market itself trending up?

A single portfolio-level switch, checked before any stock is ever looked at.

Closebench(t) < SMA200[Closebench](t) ⟹ close every position, hold cash
  • Closebench(t) — the benchmark's (default SPY) closing price on day t
  • SMA200[·] — 200-day simple moving average (regime_ma_period)

When the benchmark is below its own 200-day average, every open position is closed and the strategy sits in 100% cash until the benchmark recovers back above it — no exceptions, regardless of how well any individual stock is scoring. This is time-series momentum applied at the market level: the mechanism that keeps the strategy out of prolonged drawdowns instead of fighting them stock-by-stock.

What resets on a flip to cash last_rebalanced_stocks is cleared to an empty list. So the moment the regime turns bullish again, the new top-N selection is, by definition, a full "membership change" from empty — forcing an immediate rebalance into the new book rather than waiting for the monthly trigger.

Implements: MomentumStrategy.next(), the self.market.close[0] < self.regime_ma[0] branch in strategy.py.

04
Step 2 · Per stock, only when the regime is bullish

Stock-level trend filter

A cheap absolute-trend gate applied before any relative scoring happens.

Closei(t) > SMA200[Closei](t) ? eligible : excluded

A stock trading below its own 200-day average (ts_mom_lookback) is excluded outright, no matter how it would otherwise score — a stock can be the top cross-sectional scorer and still never make the book if it isn't itself in an uptrend. Combined with the momentum-must-be-positive check on the next slide, a stock actually has to clear two independent absolute-trend gates before its relative score is even considered.

Implements: the d.close[0] <= self._trend_sma(d): continue guard in strategy.py.

05
Step 3a · Scoring — signal 1 of 3

Momentum score

Mean percentage return across three horizons — smooths out any single lookback's noise.

Mi(t) = 13 ΣL∈{60,120,252} Closei(t) − Closei(t−L) Closei(t−L)
  • Mi(t) — momentum score for stock i on day t, as a fraction (0.10 = +10%)
  • L — one of the configured lookbacks, in trading days (~3 mo / ~6 mo / ~1 yr)

Averaging three horizons (lookbacks: [60, 120, 252]) is the intermediate-term momentum window from Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), blended across horizons so no single window's noise dominates the ranking.

Percentage return, not dollar change Each horizon is normalized by its own starting price, so the score is scale-free: a $15 stock that gained $3 (+20%) outranks a $500 stock that gained $40 (+8%). Ranking on raw dollar moves instead would systematically favour high-priced names for no economic reason. See slide 12 for this on a concrete three-stock ranking.

Implements: scoring.momentum_blend(), called from MomentumStrategy._momentum_score().

06
Step 3b · Scoring — signal 2 of 3

Frog-in-the-Pan (FIP) score

Rewards steady, "boring" grinding uptrends over the same total return achieved via a few violent jumps.

FIPi(t) = 1252 Σd=1 1[ ri(t−d) > 0 ]
  • ri(t−d) — stock i's daily return, d days back
  • 1[·] — indicator function: 1 if the condition holds, else 0
  • window: trailing 252 trading days (fip_lookback)

Simply the fraction of the last year's trading days that closed up. The academic basis (Da, Gurun & Warachka, 2014) is investor underreaction: information that arrives gradually, in a steady drip of small positive days, gets under-priced and keeps continuing — while the same total return delivered via a few large jump days tends to reflect one-off news that doesn't continue as reliably.

Implements: scoring.fip_score(), called from MomentumStrategy._fip_score().

07
Step 3c · Scoring — signal 3 of 3

Skewness score

The third standardized moment of the daily log-return distribution — how lopsided the recent moves have been.

Skewi(t) = (1/N) Σ (rᵢ − μᵢ)³ σᵢ³
  • ri — daily log returns over the trailing window; μi, σi — their mean and standard deviation
  • window: trailing 90 trading days (skewness_lookback)
  • positive skew = occasional large up-days on a base of small down-days ("lottery-like"); negative skew = the reverse
Read the sign carefully: this is a tilt, not a penalty The weight is named skewness_penalty, but it enters the combined score (next slide) with a positive sign and a positive default (0.5) — so as shipped, more positively-skewed, jumpier names score higher. To genuinely penalize jump-driven moves, as momentum-crash literature would argue for, set this coefficient negative in the config. The name describes an intent; the sign describes the behavior, and the sign is what runs.

Implements: scoring.skewness_score() (via scipy.stats.skew), called from MomentumStrategy._skewness_score().

08
Step 3d · Combining the three signals

Combined score

A simple weighted sum — no normalization between the three terms.

Scorei = wm·Mi + wf·FIPi + ws·Skewi
  • wm, wf, ws — momentum_weight, fip_weight, skewness_penalty; default 0.5 each
The three terms are summed raw, not rescaled — know their typical ranges Mi is a percentage return, typically in roughly [−0.5, 0.5]; FIPi is a fraction, strictly [0, 1]; Skewi typically runs about [−3, 3]. Because they're added without normalization, equal 0.5 weights do not mean equal influence — skew's wider natural range gives it more sway over the ranking than its weight suggests. Worth accounting for before reading the weights as a statement of intent.

Stocks are ranked by this score, highest to lowest — the ranking that feeds directly into top-N selection on the next slide.

09
Step 4 · Choosing the book

Selecting the portfolio

Three gates before a score even counts, then a simple top-N cut.

Eligiblei(t) = [Closei > SMA200,i] ∧ [Mi > 0] ∧ [historyi ≥ min required]

Only names passing all gates — index membership on this date (slide 2), the trend filter (slide 4), a positive momentum score, and enough of the stock's own real price history to fill every lookback window — get a Scorei computed at all. Eligible names are sorted by Scorei descending, and the top top_n (default 10) become the target portfolio for this bar.

Implements: the scoring loop and scores.sort(...)[: self.p.top_n] in MomentumStrategy.next().

10
Step 5 · Sizing the book

Position sizing — inverse volatility

Lower-risk names in the top-N get proportionally larger allocations — a simple risk-parity tilt.

wi = 1 / DDi Σj∈TopN 1/DDj , DDi = √ (1/N) Σ min(rᵢ, 0)²
  • wi — portfolio weight allocated to stock i
  • DDi — stock i's downside deviation: standard deviation computed only over its negative daily returns, trailing 126 days (vol_lookback) — a positive day contributes 0, not a penalty
  • a near-zero DD (e.g. no down days at all) is floored at a small constant rather than treated as "no data" — otherwise a genuinely low-risk stock would be excluded from sizing entirely instead of rewarded for it
Why downside deviation rather than plain volatility Ordinary standard deviation treats a violent move up as exactly as risky as a violent move down, which over-penalizes precisely the stocks a momentum strategy wants to hold. Downside deviation counts only the negative days — the same reasoning behind the Sortino ratio — so a stock that is "volatile" because it keeps gapping up is not punished for it.

An alternative is available as sizing_method: cap_weighted, which multiplies the numerator by an approximate market cap (shares_outstanding × close), tilting toward larger, more liquid names at equal risk. It is not the default; inverse_vol is.

Every rebalance also reserves 1% of portfolio value as a buffer — order_target_value sizes against today's close but fills at the next bar's open, and a position sized at exactly 100% of value can get margin-rejected on a single bar of price drift.

Implements: scoring.downside_deviation() and scoring.inverse_vol_weights(), called from MomentumStrategy.next() via _volatility().

When does any of this actually trade?

Rebalance mechanics

Two independent triggers — whichever fires first.

A
Membership changeAny name enters or exits the top-N versus the last rebalance → rebalance fires immediately, regardless of schedule.
B
Calendar triggerThe configured rebalance_frequency (default monthly) elapses — fires even if the top-N membership hasn't changed at all.

Trigger B exists specifically because inverse-vol weights (previous slide) go stale between membership changes — without it, the same 5 names could sit at their original sizing for months while their relative volatility drifts, since nothing would otherwise force a resize.

On a rebalance: positions no longer in the new top-N are closed first; inverse-vol weights are recomputed for the new top-N; each target position is opened/resized via order_target_value.

Implements: MomentumStrategy._is_rebalance_due() and the membership-diff check in next().

Worked example — one rebalance day, end to end

Five candidates on a single rebalance day, run through the exact formulas on slides 3–10. Numbers are illustrative but internally consistent: every score and weight below is what the shipped code computes from these inputs.

That is the scale asymmetry from slide 8 in action: with the three terms summed un-normalized, a 0.20 difference in skew outweighs a 3.2-point difference in momentum. Worth internalizing before treating the equal 0.5 weights as "all three matter equally."

Step 3 — size the book (slide 10)

Sizing is computed independently of the ranking — position order comes from score, position size comes purely from risk:

TickerDownside dev. DDᵢ1 / DDᵢWeight wᵢTarget $ (on a $100k book)
HIPX1.1%90.950.2%$49,728

LOWX3.2%31.317.3%$17,097

LOWX is the top-ranked name but gets the smallest allocation (17.3%), because it is roughly three times as volatile on the downside as HIPX. This is the intended behavior: momentum decides what to own, inverse volatility decides how much of it to own, and the two answers are deliberately allowed to disagree. Targets sum to $99,000 rather than $100,000 — the 1% buffer from slide 10 that keeps a next-bar fill from being margin-rejected.

What this is (and isn't)

A research/backtesting tool, not a trading system: no live or paper-trading execution, no walk-forward optimization or parameter search tooling, no multi-strategy portfolio composition — one strategy, one portfolio.

2Backtest Results — S&P 500, 1997–2026

One backtest, run twice: identical configuration apart from how many names are held at once (top_n = 5 and 10). Twenty-eight and a half measured years, the full S&P 500 as it actually existed on each date, benchmarked against buy-and-hold SPY over the same window.

Headline numbers

Read the two columns as a trade-off rather than a winner and a loser. top_n=5 edges out the benchmark on raw return (1,219.1% vs 1,157.1%; 9.50% vs 9.31% CAGR). top_n=10 gives up about a point of CAGR and ends behind the benchmark on cumulative return, but does so with markedly less risk — 14.3% volatility against the benchmark's 19.4%, and a −43.5% worst drawdown against −55.2%. Both land at a higher Sharpe and Sortino than buy-and-hold, which is the more durable claim here: the risk-adjusted edge is consistent across both settings, while the raw-return edge is not.

The most important number in the table is 77% Both configurations are only invested about three-quarters of the time — the regime filter holds cash for the remaining quarter. Matching a fully-invested benchmark's return while sitting out a quarter of the period is where the drawdown and volatility advantages come from. It is also the main source of risk in the other direction: the filter is a 200-day moving average, so it exits well after a top and re-enters well after a bottom.

Risk profile

The benchmark's worst drawdown is the 2007-10 → 2009-03 financial crisis, −55.2% peak-to-trough. Neither strategy configuration avoids that period, but both cut it materially (−46.7% and −43.5%). Their own worst drawdowns land in the dot-com unwind instead — mid-2000 for top_n=5, and a drawdown running into 2003 for top_n=10 — which is the expected failure mode for momentum: a sharp rotation out of the exact names that had been leading.

Turnover of ~30x a year is high, and is the single biggest caveat on these numbers. It is driven by the rebalance rules on slide 11 — any change in top-N membership triggers an immediate rebalance, and every regime flip liquidates and later rebuilds the entire book. These results model no commissions, no spread, and no slippage. At this turnover, realistic transaction costs would take a real bite out of the return column; see Limitations.

How these were produced

  • Universe: index:sp500_point_in_time — actual month-by-month S&P 500 membership, so no survivorship or look-ahead bias in the candidate list (slide 2).
  • Data: daily OHLCV via yfinance, cached in cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet. Price history is loaded for every ticker that was ever a member, ~779 of which have retrievable data. Prices are auto-adjusted for both splits and dividends, so strategy and benchmark are compared on a consistent total-return basis.
  • Benchmark: SPY, measured over the strategy's own date range.
  • Starting capital: $100,000. Orders are market orders filled at the next bar's open.
  • Everything else: stock defaults — lookbacks: [60, 120, 252], vol_lookback: 126, skewness_lookback: 90, fip_lookback: 252, ts_mom_lookback: 200, regime_ma_period: 200, weights 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.5, rebalance_frequency: monthly, sizing_method: inverse_vol.

Reproduce either run directly:

uv run momentum run configs/sp500_pit_1997_2026_n5.yaml  --output tearsheet_n5.html
uv run momentum run configs/sp500_pit_1997_2026_n10.yaml --output tearsheet_n10.html

Each writes a full quantstats tearsheet with a Summary Statistics block on top. Expect roughly 10–15 minutes per run: ~700 price feeds across ~7,200 trading days is genuinely a lot of per-bar computation, and almost none of the wall time is data fetching once the cache is warm.

Limitations — read before quoting any number above

1. No transaction costs, spread, or slippage are modeled At ~30x annual turnover this is the most consequential omission on the page. A few basis points per trade compounds into a material drag over 28 years.
2. Constituent history is reconstructed, not vendor-supplied Monthly membership comes from a community-maintained reconstruction of the index, not an authoritative point-in-time vendor feed. It is dramatically better than applying today's list to the past, but it is not audited data, and the pre-2001 portion is the least reliable stretch.
3. Roughly a third of historical constituents have no retrievable price data Companies that were delisted, acquired, or went bankrupt long ago frequently return nothing from Yahoo Finance. Those names are skipped, which means the deepest failures in the index's history are under-represented in the candidate pool — a residual bias pointing in the optimistic direction.
4. Two settings on one history is not a validated strategy These are two runs over a single (long) period. Nothing here has been walk-forward validated or tested out-of-sample on another index or market, and no parameter on slides 5–10 has been swept. Treat the table as a measurement of this implementation, not as evidence about momentum investing generally.
5. Cash earns nothing while the regime filter is out of the market For 23% of the period the book sits in cash at a 0% risk-free rate. Over 1998–2026 — which includes stretches of 5%+ short rates — a real implementation parking that cash in T-bills would have earned meaningfully more than these figures credit it with. This one biases the results pessimistically, unlike the others on this list.

3Code Structure & How to Run It

The package was refactored from a single 300+ line script (raw/main.py, kept for reference) into a proper src-layout Python package with the trading math separated from the backtrader execution engine, so the math can be unit tested without spinning up a backtest.

Package layout

momentum/ ├── pyproject.toml, README.md ├── configs/ │ └── default.yaml # sample run config ├── src/momentum/ │ ├── config.py # RunConfig / StrategyConfig dataclasses + YAML loader │ ├── universe.py # static, live index, or point-in-time constituent resolution │ ├── data.py # yfinance fetch + local parquet cache │ ├── scoring.py # pure math: momentum, FIP, skewness, combined score, inverse-vol │ ├── indicators.py # backtrader Indicator wrappers (kept; strategy computes inline) │ ├── strategy.py # MomentumStrategy(bt.Strategy) │ ├── backtest.py # builds Cerebro, wires feeds + strategy, extracts returns │ ├── reporting.py # quantstats tearsheet generation │ └── cli.py # `momentum run configs/default.yaml` ├── tests/ # one test module per src module, plus a smoke test └── raw/ # original single-file prototype this was built from

Module reference

ModuleResponsibilityNotes
scoring.py Pure momentum/FIP/skewness/inverse-vol math Zero backtrader imports — importable and unit-testable standalone. This is the module to point to when asked "what's actually novel here."
strategy.py MomentumStrategy(bt.Strategy) — the regime filter, trend filter, scoring loop, position sizing, and order placement The only place that touches backtrader's per-bar next() lifecycle and broker/position APIs. Per-stock signals are computed on demand from raw price windows inside next() rather than as registered indicators, which keeps each stock's readiness independent of every other stock's. Also the only place that can track turnover (notify_order() accumulates filled traded value) and daily portfolio value (value_history), neither of which is recoverable from a returns series alone.
config.py RunConfig / StrategyConfig dataclasses; YAML loader with validation Validates required top-level fields and rebalance_frequency (only None or "monthly") at load time — fails fast instead of at strategy runtime.
universe.py Resolves a ticker list from config Three sources: static (inline list), index:sp500 (live Wikipedia scrape of today's members), and index:sp500_point_in_time, which returns every ticker ever in the index plus a month-end → member-set lookup so the strategy can restrict each rebalance to the membership that actually existed then (slide 2).
data.py Per-ticker OHLCV fetch via yfinance, backed by a git-tracked parquet store sharded across four files (cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet); also caches shares outstanding (cache_data/shares_outstanding.parquet) for cap-weighted sizing Long-format store (ticker, Date, OHLCV columns) split across 4 shards keyed by crc32(ticker) % 4, loaded once per process. Coverage-based, not time-based: a ticker's rows are reused only if their date range already covers the request; otherwise it downloads, merges, and rewrites only that ticker's shard. Checked into git specifically so a fresh clone has working data for any ticker/range someone has already fetched, with no network call. Tickers Yahoo has no data for at all (long-delisted names, dead symbols) are recorded in cache_data/no_data_tickers.parquet so they aren't re-attempted every run — with ~1,200 historical constituents, ~429 of which are permanently unavailable, that alone saves minutes per run. Shares outstanding is a single current snapshot per ticker (via fast_info), used as shares × close(t) to approximate historical market cap — cheap, but technically uses information not available on past rebalance dates.
backtest.py Orchestration: builds Cerebro, adds benchmark as datas[0] and universe as datas[1:], runs, and converts the strategy's recorded daily portfolio value into a returns series Missing per-ticker data is logged and skipped, not fatal; missing benchmark data is fatal. Validates every feed has enough trading days for the configured lookbacks (_min_required_bars) before handing it to backtrader. Also aligns every ticker onto the benchmark's own trading-day calendar (_align_to_calendar) so all feeds are the same length regardless of listing date, and passes each ticker's real listing position to the strategy so a stock only becomes eligible once it has genuinely accumulated enough of its own history.
reporting.py Generates the quantstats tearsheet, then injects a custom Summary Statistics block into the same HTML file Backtest period, absolute/relative total return, CAGR (strategy vs. benchmark), Sharpe ratio, and annualized turnover — turnover isn't something quantstats can compute (it only sees returns, not trades), so it's tracked separately in strategy.py. Replaced a ~230-line hand-rolled Excel reporting pipeline in the original prototype.
cli.py argparse-based entrypoint: momentum run <config> [--output path] Registered as a console script (project.scripts in pyproject.toml), so it runs as momentum once installed.

Running it

# install dependencies
uv sync

# run a backtest against the default config
uv run momentum run configs/default.yaml

# write the tearsheet somewhere else
uv run momentum run configs/default.yaml --output results/tearsheet.html

A run reads OHLCV data for every ticker in the universe plus the benchmark from cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet (checked into the repo) wherever it's already covered there, downloading and adding only what's missing, then executes the strategy over the configured date range and writes an HTML performance tearsheet (default tearsheet.html) comparing the strategy's returns to the benchmark's — with a Summary Statistics block (backtest period, absolute/relative return, CAGR, Sharpe, annualized turnover) inserted at the top of the same file, ahead of the full quantstats report.

4Design Notes

The decisions below are the ones that aren't obvious from reading the code, plus the behaviors most likely to surprise someone modifying it. Read this section if you're about to change something; skip it if you just want to run a backtest.

Key design decisions

  • Scoring math is framework-agnostic. scoring.py has zero backtrader imports specifically so the strategy's core logic can be unit tested in milliseconds, without a full backtest run.
  • Dual rebalance trigger, added deliberately. The original prototype only rebalanced on membership change, which meant inverse-vol weights went stale for names that stayed in the top-N for months. A monthly trigger was added alongside the membership-change trigger to keep sizing current.
  • Cache is git-tracked, sharded, and coverage-based rather than time-based. data.py keeps every ticker's OHLCV history in cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet (long format: ticker, Date, OHLCV), checked into the repo so a fresh clone has working data for anything already fetched — no network call needed. A ticker's rows are reused only when their date range already covers what's requested, not on a TTL, so re-running the same backtest twice does zero network calls the second time either way. Rows are assigned to a shard by crc32(ticker) so a run that touches a few tickers rewrites only the shards those tickers live in, rather than the whole dataset — which keeps each commit's diff proportional to what actually changed, and keeps every file well under hosting size limits. Prices are stored as float32 with zstd compression; the discarded precision is far below any price's real significance.
  • Config validated at load time. Both missing required fields and an invalid rebalance_frequency raise immediately in config.py, rather than surfacing as a confusing failure deep inside a multi-minute backtest run.
  • Per-ticker data gaps degrade gracefully; benchmark gaps don't. A missing/empty ticker is logged and skipped — the backtest proceeds with the rest of the universe. A missing benchmark is a hard failure, because the regime filter has no fallback.
  • Turnover has to be tracked at the trade level, not derived from returns. Every other headline stat (CAGR, Sharpe, total return) is a pure function of the returns series and comes straight from quantstats. Turnover needs actual traded dollar value, which only exists inside backtrader's order lifecycle — hence MomentumStrategy.notify_order() accumulating it, threaded back through run_backtest()'s return value into reporting.py.
  • Feed length is validated up front, then normalized. _min_required_bars rejects any feed too short to ever satisfy the configured lookbacks — a clear error for the benchmark, a logged skip for a ticker. Everything that survives is then reindexed onto the benchmark's trading-day calendar so all feeds share one clock, with each ticker's real listing date tracked separately to gate eligibility. Uniform feed lengths keep one stock's readiness from interacting with another's.
  • Per-stock signals are computed inline, not registered as indicators. Momentum, FIP, skewness, volatility, and the trend SMA are all computed on demand inside next() from raw price windows. Registering hundreds of per-stock indicator objects instead would couple every stock's warmup to every other stock's, which is a poor fit for a universe whose members list changes over decades.
  • The benchmark is measured over the strategy's own window. The strategy has no returns until its regime SMA warms up; comparing it against a benchmark measured from an earlier start date would silently hand the benchmark months of free return. reporting.py clips the benchmark to the strategy's date range before computing any comparison stat.

Gotchas worth knowing cold

1. skewness_penalty currently rewards, not penalizes, skew See slide 7 — the coefficient is added with a positive sign and a positive default weight.
2. The three score terms are summed without normalization Equal 0.5 weights do not mean equal influence — skew's natural range is several times wider than momentum's, so it moves the ranking more than its weight implies. See slide 8, and slide 12 for a concrete ranking flip caused by a 0.20 difference in skew.
3. Ranking and sizing deliberately disagree The top-ranked name routinely receives the smallest allocation, because sizing is driven purely by downside deviation and ignores the score entirely (slide 10). That's intended, not a bug — but it surprises people reading a position list for the first time.
4. Sizing uses a 99%-of-value buffer Because order_target_value sizes against today's close but fills at the next bar's open, a position sized at exactly 100% of portfolio value can get margin-rejected on a single bar of price drift. The 1% reserve is a deliberate, documented workaround, not an arbitrary constant.
5. Only "monthly" is a valid non-null rebalance frequency today config.py enforces this explicitly; strategy.py's own _is_rebalance_due would otherwise raise a less-clear ValueError deep inside a run for any other string.
6. The date range must cover at least ~253 trading days Driven by fip_lookback + 1; a shorter range raises a clear ValueError (benchmark) or skips the ticker (per-name) instead of an opaque crash — see the note under §3 Running with S&P 500.
7. Cash earns 0% while the regime filter is out of the market There is no risk-free rate credited to idle cash, and the strategy sits in cash roughly a quarter of the time (§2). Every reported return is understated by whatever that cash would have earned in T-bills.

Questions you might get, and how to answer them

Why separate scoring.py from strategy.py?

Testability and reuse — the same momentum/FIP/skewness math could back a different execution engine or a research notebook without dragging in backtrader.

Why inverse-volatility sizing instead of equal weight?

It's a simple risk-parity tilt: among the top-N momentum names, it reduces the influence of the single most volatile holding on portfolio swings, without needing a full covariance-based optimizer.

What happens in a sideways, choppy market?

The regime filter only reacts to the benchmark's 200-day trend, so a genuinely choppy-but-flat market won't trigger the cash switch — the strategy could still whipsaw in and out of individual names as membership changes, since there's no minimum holding period beyond the periodic/membership rebalance logic.

Why hold cash instead of shorting or rotating into bonds in a bear market?

Simplicity, and because the regime filter is a blunt instrument. A 200-day moving average is late by construction — it exits after a decline is well underway and re-enters after a recovery is well underway. Layering shorts on top of a signal with that much lag adds a way to lose money in exactly the whipsaw conditions the filter handles worst. Rotating into bonds is more defensible and is already possible today via the static universe, which can include TLT/GLD and let momentum choose between asset classes directly.

If you had to name the single biggest risk in the reported results, what would it be?

Transaction costs. Turnover runs around 30x a year and nothing in the backtest models commissions, spread, or slippage (§2 Limitations). The risk-adjusted edge over buy-and-hold is real in the numbers as computed, but it is not large enough that it can be assumed to survive realistic trading frictions — measuring that is the most valuable next piece of work on this codebase.

What's the biggest methodology caveat in the strategy logic itself?

skewness_penalty rewards rather than penalizes skew under the shipped defaults (slide 7), so the strategy currently tilts toward jumpy, lottery-like names. That's a defensible configuration choice, but it's the opposite of what the parameter's name implies and the opposite of what momentum-crash literature would suggest — worth a deliberate decision rather than inheriting it by default.
