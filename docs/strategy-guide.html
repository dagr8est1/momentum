Momentum Investing Strategy

A cross-sectional + time-series momentum stock-rotation strategy, backtested with backtrader and packaged as an installable, config-driven Python tool. This doc explains what the strategy does and why, then how the codebase is put together — enough to walk through either half in an interview.

1How Momentum Investing Works Here

Twelve slides, in the order the code actually executes them: from "what is this strategy, conceptually" down to the exact formulas and the mechanics of when and how the portfolio changes. Every formula below is a direct transcription of a function in scoring.py, indicators.py, or strategy.py — not a textbook approximation — including two places where the code's actual math differs from what its variable names imply.

Core idea→ Universe→ Regime filter→ Trend filter→ Momentum→ FIP→ Skew→ Combine→ Select top-N→ Size→ Rebalance
01
Foundations

The core idea

Rotate into a small book of trending winners, and go to cash when the market itself is trending down. Three ideas from the momentum literature, combined — nothing invented from scratch.

Cross-sectional

Rank stocks against each other every period. Only the strongest relative performers (top 5) are held — the classic Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) momentum effect.

Time-series

Each holding must also be trending up in absolute terms (price above its own 200-day average). Relative and absolute trend must agree — Antonacci's "dual momentum."

Regime-aware

A market-level trend filter can flatten the entire book to cash. The strategy doesn't try to stock-pick its way through a bear market.

Implements: the overall shape of MomentumStrategy.next() in strategy.py.

02
Foundations

The universe

What's eligible to be ranked in the first place.

configs/default.yaml ships a static list of ~140 tickers spanning mega-cap single names (tech, healthcare, financials, industrials, staples, energy), sector SPDRs (XLK, XLF, …), factor/style ETFs (MTUM, QUAL, VLUE, USMV), thematic ETFs (ARKK, ICLN, SOXX), international/EM (EFA, EEM, FXI), and bonds/commodities (TLT, GLD, USO). The point of the breadth: momentum can rotate into whatever regime is actually working — equities, gold, bonds — not just stocks.

The universe is pluggable: source: static uses that inline list; source: index:sp500 instead scrapes the live S&P 500 constituent table from Wikipedia at run time (universe.py) — see §2 Running with S&P 500.

03
Step 1 · Every bar, before anything else

Regime filter — is the market itself trending up?

A single portfolio-level switch, checked before any stock is ever looked at.

Closebench(t) < SMA200[Closebench](t) ⟹ close every position, hold cash
  • Closebench(t) — the benchmark's (default SPY) closing price on day t
  • SMA200[·] — 200-day simple moving average (regime_ma_period)

When the benchmark is below its own 200-day average, every open position is closed and the strategy sits in 100% cash until the benchmark recovers back above it — no exceptions, regardless of how well any individual stock is scoring. This is time-series momentum applied at the market level: the mechanism that keeps the strategy out of prolonged drawdowns instead of fighting them stock-by-stock.

What resets on a flip to cash last_rebalanced_stocks is cleared to an empty list. So the moment the regime turns bullish again, the new top-5 selection is, by definition, a full "membership change" from empty — forcing an immediate rebalance into the new book rather than waiting for the monthly trigger.

Implements: MomentumStrategy.next(), the self.market.close[0] < self.regime_ma[0] branch in strategy.py.

04
Step 2 · Per stock, only when the regime is bullish

Stock-level trend filter

A cheap absolute-trend gate applied before any relative scoring happens.

Closei(t) > SMA200[Closei](t) ? eligible : excluded

A stock trading below its own 200-day average (ts_mom_lookback) is excluded outright, no matter how it would otherwise score — a stock can be the top cross-sectional scorer and still never make the book if it isn't itself in an uptrend. Combined with the momentum-must-be-positive check on the next slide, a stock actually has to clear two independent absolute-trend gates before its relative score is even considered.

Implements: the d.close[0] <= self.indicators[...]["ts_mom"][0]: continue guard in strategy.py.

05
Step 3a · Scoring — signal 1 of 3

Momentum score

Mean percentage return across three horizons — smooths out any single lookback's noise.

Mi(t) = 13 ΣL∈{60,120,252} Closei(t) − Closei(t−L) Closei(t−L)
  • Mi(t) — momentum score for stock i on day t, as a fraction (0.10 = +10%)
  • L — one of the configured lookbacks, in trading days (~3 mo / ~6 mo / ~1 yr)

Averaging three horizons (lookbacks: [60, 120, 252]) is the intermediate-term momentum window from Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), blended across horizons so no single window's noise dominates the ranking.

Fixed — this was absolute price momentum, not percentage return momentum_blend() originally computed Close(t) − Close(t−L) directly on raw prices, never dividing by the starting price — a $500 stock that moved $40 scored higher than a $15 stock that moved $3, even if the $15 stock's percentage gain was actually larger, biasing the ranking toward high-priced names. Normalizing to a percentage return (shown above) fixed this. Measured effect on the real S&P 500 backtest (§2): Sharpe ratio −0.03 → 0.08, CAGR −0.7% → +0.3%, and annualized turnover dropped 4.06x → 2.42x/year — a more stable ranking traded less. Still well behind the benchmark's 8.8% CAGR, so this was a real contributor but not the whole story. See the worked example on slide 12 for the before/after on a concrete ranking.

Implements: scoring.momentum_blend(), called from MomentumStrategy._momentum_score().

06
Step 3b · Scoring — signal 2 of 3

Frog-in-the-Pan (FIP) score

Rewards steady, "boring" grinding uptrends over the same total return achieved via a few violent jumps.

FIPi(t) = 1252 Σd=1 1[ ri(t−d) > 0 ]
  • ri(t−d) — stock i's daily return, d days back
  • 1[·] — indicator function: 1 if the condition holds, else 0
  • window: trailing 252 trading days (fip_lookback)

Simply the fraction of the last year's trading days that closed up. The academic basis (Da, Gurun & Warachka, 2014) is investor underreaction: information that arrives gradually, in a steady drip of small positive days, gets under-priced and keeps continuing — while the same total return delivered via a few large jump days tends to reflect one-off news that doesn't continue as reliably.

Implements: scoring.fip_score(), wrapped by the FrogInThePan indicator in indicators.py.

07
Step 3c · Scoring — signal 3 of 3

Skewness score

The third standardized moment of the daily log-return distribution — how lopsided the recent moves have been.

Skewi(t) = (1/N) Σ (rᵢ − μᵢ)³ σᵢ³
  • ri — daily log returns over the trailing window; μi, σi — their mean and standard deviation
  • window: trailing 90 trading days (skewness_lookback)
  • positive skew = occasional large up-days on a base of small down-days ("lottery-like"); negative skew = the reverse
Naming vs. behavior — worth knowing cold The weight for this term is called skewness_penalty, but it's added to the combined score (next slide) with a positive sign and a positive default value (0.5) — so under current defaults, more positively-skewed, jumpier return distributions score higher, not lower. If the intent was to genuinely penalize jump-driven moves (as the name implies, and as momentum-crash literature would argue), this coefficient needs to be negative. As shipped, it's a configurable tilt, not a penalty.

Implements: scoring.skewness_score() (via scipy.stats.skew), wrapped by the RollingSkewness indicator in indicators.py.

08
Step 3d · Combining the three signals

Combined score

A simple weighted sum — no normalization between the three terms.

Scorei = wm·Mi + wf·FIPi + ws·Skewi
  • wm, wf, ws — momentum_weight, fip_weight, skewness_penalty; default 0.5 each
Improved, not fully solved — the three terms still aren't on identical scales Now that Mi is a percentage return (slide 5), it typically sits in roughly [−0.5, 0.5] for a 3-year window — much closer to FIPi's [0, 1] and Skewi's roughly [−3, 3] than the old raw-dollar version, which could run into the tens. It's no longer 15–20× out of scale, but the three terms still aren't formally normalized (e.g. z-scored) against each other, so Skewi's wider typical range can still outweigh the other two more than the equal 0.5 weights might suggest. The slide-12 worked example shows the before/after concretely.

Stocks are ranked by this score, highest to lowest — the ranking that feeds directly into top-N selection on the next slide.

09
Step 4 · Choosing the book

Selecting the portfolio

Three gates before a score even counts, then a simple top-N cut.

Eligiblei(t) = [Closei > SMA200,i] ∧ [Mi > 0] ∧ [historyi ≥ min required]

Only names passing all three — the trend filter (slide 4), a positive momentum score, and enough price history to compute every indicator — get a Scorei computed at all. Eligible names are sorted by Scorei descending, and the top top_n (default 5) become the target portfolio for this bar.

Implements: the scoring loop and scores.sort(...)[: self.p.top_n] in MomentumStrategy.next().

10
Step 5 · Sizing the book

Position sizing — inverse volatility

Lower-volatility names in the top-N get proportionally larger allocations — a simple risk-parity tilt.

wi = 1 / σᵢ Σj∈TopN 1/σⱼ
  • wi — portfolio weight allocated to stock i
  • σi — standard deviation of stock i's daily returns over the trailing 126 days (vol_lookback); zero/negative σ is excluded from the sum rather than dividing by zero
Fixed — this was standard deviation of the price level, not of returns σi originally came from bt.indicators.StdDev(d.close, period=vol_lookback) — the standard deviation of the raw closing price, not of daily percentage returns (the usual definition of volatility in finance). A stock in a strong, steady uptrend racked up a large price-level standard deviation purely from the trend itself, even with low day-to-day riskiness, while a flat, choppy low-priced stock could look artificially "low volatility" just because its price level was small — systematically favoring low-priced names. Fixed by computing σi on daily returns instead (bt.indicators.StdDev(bt.indicators.PercentChange(d.close, period=1), period=vol_lookback)). Measured effect on the real S&P 500 backtest, on top of the momentum fix (§2): Sharpe ratio 0.08 → 0.28, CAGR +0.3% → +1.4%, turnover kept dropping (2.42x → 1.79x/year). See the before/after sizing table on slide 12.

Every rebalance also reserves 1% of portfolio value as a buffer — order_target_value sizes against today's close but fills at the next bar's open, and a position sized at exactly 100% of value can get margin-rejected on a single bar of price drift.

Implements: scoring.inverse_vol_weights(), called from MomentumStrategy.next().

11
When does any of this actually trade?

Rebalance mechanics

Two independent triggers — whichever fires first.

A
Membership changeAny name enters or exits the top-N versus the last rebalance → rebalance fires immediately, regardless of schedule.
B
Calendar triggerThe configured rebalance_frequency (default monthly) elapses — fires even if the top-N membership hasn't changed at all.

Trigger B exists specifically because inverse-vol weights (previous slide) go stale between membership changes — without it, the same 5 names could sit at their original sizing for months while their relative volatility drifts, since nothing would otherwise force a resize.

On a rebalance: positions no longer in the new top-N are closed first; inverse-vol weights are recomputed for the new top-N; each target position is opened/resized via order_target_value.

Implements: MomentumStrategy._is_rebalance_due() and the membership-diff check in next().

Worked example — both fixes, before and after

LOWX has the largest percentage gain of the three (+16.3%). Under the original raw-difference formula it ranked last — its $2.10 move was dwarfed by HIPX's $38.00, and FIP/Skew couldn't offset a 15–20× gap in the momentum term. After normalizing to percentage returns, the ranking flips: LOWX moves from 3rd to 1st, and HIPX drops from 1st to 2nd. This is the actual mechanism behind the real-run improvement on slide 5 — the fix doesn't just shrink the numbers, it changes which stocks get bought.

Suppose all three make the top-N (sizing runs independently of the momentum ranking above). Under the original price-level formula, LOWX got 80% of the book's capital simply because its low share price mechanically produced a small price-level standard deviation — even though, measured properly on daily returns, LOWX is actually the most volatile (choppy, low-priced) of the three, not the least. After the fix, its weight drops to 22% — a complete reversal, not just a smaller number. This was the second, independent fix (slide 10): fixing momentum changed who gets picked; fixing sizing changed how much they get, and both measurably improved the real backtest.

What this is (and isn't)

A research/backtesting tool, not a trading system: no live or paper-trading execution, no walk-forward optimization or parameter search tooling, no multi-strategy portfolio composition — one strategy, one portfolio.

2Code Structure & How to Run It

The package was refactored from a single 300+ line script (raw/main.py, kept for reference) into a proper src-layout Python package with the trading math separated from the backtrader execution engine, so the math can be unit tested without spinning up a backtest.

Package layout

momentum/ ├── pyproject.toml, README.md ├── configs/ │ └── default.yaml # sample run config ├── src/momentum/ │ ├── config.py # RunConfig / StrategyConfig dataclasses + YAML loader │ ├── universe.py # static list or dynamic index-constituent resolution │ ├── data.py # yfinance fetch + local parquet cache │ ├── scoring.py # pure math: momentum, FIP, skewness, combined score, inverse-vol │ ├── indicators.py # backtrader Indicator wrappers around scoring.py │ ├── strategy.py # MomentumStrategy(bt.Strategy) │ ├── backtest.py # builds Cerebro, wires feeds + strategy, extracts returns │ ├── reporting.py # quantstats tearsheet generation │ └── cli.py # `momentum run configs/default.yaml` ├── tests/ # one test module per src module, plus a smoke test └── raw/ # original single-file prototype this was built from

Module reference

ModuleResponsibilityNotes
scoring.py Pure momentum/FIP/skewness/inverse-vol math Zero backtrader imports — importable and unit-testable standalone. This is the module to point to when asked "what's actually novel here."
indicators.py RollingSkewness and FrogInThePan, thin bt.Indicator subclasses Each just windows the price series and calls into scoring.py per bar — no math lives here.
strategy.py MomentumStrategy(bt.Strategy) — the regime filter, trend filter, scoring loop, and order placement The only place that touches backtrader's per-bar next() lifecycle and broker/position APIs. Also the only place that can track turnover — notify_order() accumulates every filled order's traded value, since that information doesn't exist in a returns series alone.
config.py RunConfig / StrategyConfig dataclasses; YAML loader with validation Validates required top-level fields and rebalance_frequency (only None or "monthly") at load time — fails fast instead of at strategy runtime.
universe.py Resolves a ticker list from config source: static (inline list) or source: index:sp500 (live Wikipedia scrape).
data.py Per-ticker OHLCV fetch via yfinance, backed by one combined, git-tracked parquet file (cache_data/prices.parquet) instead of per-ticker files Long-format store (ticker, Date, OHLCV columns), loaded once per process and reused for every ticker in the run. Coverage-based, not time-based: a ticker's rows are reused only if their date range already covers the request; otherwise it downloads, merges, and rewrites the whole file. Checked into git specifically so a fresh clone has working data for any ticker/range someone has already fetched, with no network call. Also flattens the MultiIndex columns yfinance sometimes returns.
backtest.py Orchestration: builds Cerebro, adds benchmark as datas[0] and universe as datas[1:], runs, extracts the TimeReturn analyzer Missing per-ticker data is logged and skipped, not fatal; missing benchmark data is fatal. Also validates every feed has enough trading days for the configured lookbacks (_min_required_bars) before handing it to backtrader — a too-short benchmark raises a clear error, a too-short ticker (e.g. a recent IPO/spinoff) is logged and skipped, same as missing data.
reporting.py Generates the quantstats tearsheet, then injects a custom Summary Statistics block into the same HTML file Backtest period, absolute/relative total return, CAGR (strategy vs. benchmark), Sharpe ratio, and annualized turnover — turnover isn't something quantstats can compute (it only sees returns, not trades), so it's tracked separately in strategy.py. Replaced a ~230-line hand-rolled Excel reporting pipeline in the original prototype.
cli.py argparse-based entrypoint: momentum run <config> [--output path] Registered as a console script (project.scripts in pyproject.toml), so it runs as momentum once installed.

Running it

# install dependencies
uv sync

# run a backtest against the default config
uv run momentum run configs/default.yaml

# write the tearsheet somewhere else
uv run momentum run configs/default.yaml --output results/tearsheet.html

# run the test suite
uv run pytest

A run reads OHLCV data for every ticker in the universe plus the benchmark from cache_data/prices.parquet (checked into the repo) wherever it's already covered there, downloading and adding only what's missing, then executes the strategy over the configured date range and writes an HTML performance tearsheet (default tearsheet.html) comparing the strategy's returns to the benchmark's — with a Summary Statistics block (backtest period, absolute/relative return, CAGR, Sharpe, annualized turnover) inserted at the top of the same file, ahead of the full quantstats report.

Running against the S&P 500 universe

configs/default.yaml ships with a static ~140-ticker universe. To backtest against the current S&P 500 constituents instead, set universe.source to index:sp500 — universe.py resolves this by scraping the live constituent table from Wikipedia at run time, so no ticker list needs to be maintained by hand. 

benchmark: SPY
start_date: "2022-01-01"
end_date: "2024-12-31"
cache_dir: cache_data

universe:
  source: index:sp500

strategy:
  lookbacks: [60, 120, 252]
  top_n: 5
  vol_lookback: 126
  skewness_lookback: 90
  fip_lookback: 252
  ts_mom_lookback: 200
  regime_ma_period: 200
  momentum_weight: 0.5
  fip_weight: 0.5
  skewness_penalty: 0.5
  rebalance_frequency: monthly

Saved as configs/sp500.yaml, run it the same way as any other config:

uv run momentum run configs/sp500.yaml --output tearsheet_sp500.html
Verified: ~3 minutes end-to-end, 493/500 tickers used This exact config was run against the live S&P 500 constituent list, with three years of history for all ~500 tickers already sitting in the committed cache_data/prices.parquet — most of the ~3 minute wall time is the backtest's own per-bar computation across ~493 stocks, not data fetching. 7 tickers were skipped automatically, each logged as a warning rather than failing the run: a few no longer resolve on Yahoo Finance (ticker changes since the snapshot), and a few recently-listed spinoffs/IPOs (e.g. GEV, SOLV, RDDT) don't yet have the ~253 trading days of history the default lookbacks require — see the _min_required_bars check in backtest.py, added specifically to turn that case into a clean skip instead of a crash. On a fresh clone with no cache at all, expect this same run to take noticeably longer the first time, purely on data-fetch time — and whatever it fetches gets added to cache_data/prices.parquet for next time.

Two real fixes, two real improvements — and still a real, honest gap Each column is the exact same config, re-run after fixing one more thing: momentum → percentage return, then sizing → return-based volatility. Both independently improved every metric — Sharpe went from negative to 0.28 (roughly half the benchmark's, up from essentially zero), CAGR climbed from −0.7% to +1.4%, and turnover fell by more than half as the rankings and sizing became less noise-driven. These are genuine, measured, non-trivial improvements, not rounding noise.

They're also nowhere near enough to close a ~7.4-point CAGR gap to the benchmark. At this point, the honest move is to stop tuning: the two fixes made were bug fixes (units the code's own logic didn't intend), not parameter tweaks chosen because they happened to help this backtest — repeatedly adjusting lookbacks, weights, or top_n against this exact 2022–2024 window to chase a better number would be overfitting to one historical period, not genuine improvement, and this tool explicitly excludes that kind of parameter search (§1 Non-goals). 2022 was also a documented difficult year for momentum strategies broadly (a rate-shock regime), so some of the remaining gap may be the market environment rather than anything left to fix here. The honest conclusion from one 3-year window over one universe is "two real bugs materially hurt performance, now fixed" — not "this strategy now beats the market" or "momentum doesn't work." Answering either of those needs walk-forward validation across multiple periods, which this tool deliberately doesn't do.
  • Needs network access to both en.wikipedia.org (constituent scrape) and Yahoo Finance (price data).
  • A handful of tickers will fail to fetch or have too little history; both cases are logged and skipped rather than aborting the run.
  • Don't shrink the date range below ~14 months to "test faster" — the default fip_lookback: 252 needs 253 trading days of history per ticker, and a single calendar year only has ~251. A too-short range now raises a clear ValueError naming the shortfall instead of crashing deep inside backtrader with an opaque IndexError (a real bug this guide's own S&P 500 testing turned up — fixed and covered by test_backtest_smoke.py). To iterate faster, shrink the universe (fewer tickers) instead of the date range.

Testing strategy

  • test_scoring.py — pure math: combined-score weighting, FIP ratio on known positive/negative-day sequences, skewness on a known return series, inverse-vol normalization including the zero-volatility edge case. No backtrader involved.
  • test_config.py — YAML loads into the expected dataclass shape; invalid/missing fields raise clear errors at load time.
  • test_backtest_smoke.py — a small end-to-end run (benchmark + a handful of tickers, short date range, no live network calls) asserting the pipeline completes, produces a non-empty returns series, and writes a tearsheet. Also covers the two insufficient-history cases: a too-short benchmark raises ValueError, a too-short ticker is logged and skipped. This is the regression guard for cross-module wiring.
  • test_data.py, test_universe.py, test_indicators.py, test_strategy.py, test_cli.py, test_reporting.py — one module per source file, all network calls mocked/monkeypatched.

All 39 tests pass with uv run pytest; no test hits the network.

3Interview Prep

The points below are the ones most likely to come up if someone probes "why is it built this way" rather than just "what does it do."

Key design decisions

  • Scoring math is framework-agnostic. scoring.py has zero backtrader imports specifically so the strategy's core logic can be unit tested in milliseconds, without a full backtest run.
  • Dual rebalance trigger, added deliberately. The original prototype only rebalanced on membership change, which meant inverse-vol weights went stale for names that stayed in the top 5 for months. A monthly trigger was added alongside the membership-change trigger to keep sizing current.
  • Cache is one combined, git-tracked file, and coverage-based rather than time-based. data.py keeps every ticker's OHLCV history in a single cache_data/prices.parquet (long format: ticker, Date, OHLCV), checked into the repo so a fresh clone has working data for anything already fetched — no network call needed. A ticker's rows are reused only when their date range already covers what's requested, not on a TTL, so re-running the same backtest twice does zero network calls the second time either way.
  • Config validated at load time. Both missing required fields and an invalid rebalance_frequency raise immediately in config.py, rather than surfacing as a confusing failure deep inside a multi-minute backtest run.
  • Per-ticker data gaps degrade gracefully; benchmark gaps don't. A missing/empty ticker is logged and skipped — the backtest proceeds with the rest of the universe. A missing benchmark is a hard failure, because the regime filter has no fallback.
  • Turnover has to be tracked at the trade level, not derived from returns. Every other headline stat (CAGR, Sharpe, total return) is a pure function of the returns series and comes straight from quantstats. Turnover needs actual traded dollar value, which only exists inside backtrader's order lifecycle — hence MomentumStrategy.notify_order() accumulating it, threaded back through run_backtest()'s return value into reporting.py.
  • Data-length validation added after a real bug. Testing the S&P 500 universe against a too-short date range crashed with a raw backtrader IndexError — the fix (backtest.py::_min_required_bars) validates every feed's length against the configured lookbacks upfront, converting that into either a clear error (benchmark) or a graceful skip (ticker), matching the existing missing-data philosophy rather than inventing a new one.

Gotchas worth knowing cold

1. skewness_penalty currently rewards, not penalizes, skew See slide 7 — the coefficient is added with a positive sign and a positive default weight.
2. Fixed: momentum used to be a raw price difference, not a percentage return Was dollar-scale (slide 8 discusses the residual scale gap vs. FIP/Skew); now a percentage return (slide 5). Questions you might get, and how to answer them

Why separate scoring.py from strategy.py?

Testability and reuse — the same momentum/FIP/skewness math could back a different execution engine or a research notebook without dragging in backtrader.

Why inverse-volatility sizing instead of equal weight?

It's a simple risk-parity tilt: among the top-5 momentum names, it reduces the influence of the single most volatile holding on portfolio swings, without needing a full covariance-based optimizer.

What happens in a sideways, choppy market?

The regime filter only reacts to the benchmark's 200-day trend, so a genuinely choppy-but-flat market won't trigger the cash switch — the strategy could still whipsaw in and out of individual names as membership changes, since there's no minimum holding period beyond the periodic/membership rebalance logic.

You mentioned momentum and sizing used to have unit bugs — what happened when you fixed them?

Fixed one at a time and re-ran the exact same S&P 500 config after each (§2 has the full table). Momentum → percentage return: Sharpe −0.03 → 0.08, CAGR −0.7% → +0.3%. Sizing → return-based volatility, on top of that: Sharpe 0.08 → 0.28, CAGR +0.3% → +1.4%. Both real, measured, non-trivial improvements — and still ~7.4 points of CAGR behind the benchmark.

Both known bugs are fixed and it's still losing to the benchmark by a lot. What now?

Stop tuning parameters against this one backtest — that's overfitting to a single 2022–2024 window dressed up as "improvement," and it's exactly what this tool's non-goals rule out (§1). The honest next steps are structural, not cosmetic: add transaction-cost/slippage modeling (turnover is measured at 1.79x/year now, so costs aren't negligible), add walk-forward validation across multiple periods before drawing any conclusion about the strategy itself, and separately note that 2022 was a documented difficult year for momentum strategies broadly — some of the remaining gap may be the market regime, not the code.

If you had to name the single biggest remaining methodology risk, what would it be?

skewness_penalty still rewards rather than penalizes skew (slide 7) — the one identified unit/sign issue that hasn't been measured yet. It's lower-confidence than the two fixed bugs, though: skew is a smaller term in the combined score even after the momentum fix, so the expected effect size is probably smaller too.
