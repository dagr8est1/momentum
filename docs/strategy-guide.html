Momentum Investing Strategy

A strategy that rotates money into whichever stocks have recently been rising fastest, backtested with backtrader and packaged as an installable, config-driven Python tool. This doc explains exactly what the strategy does and why, every formula it uses, how it performed over a 28-year survivorship-bias-free S&P 500 backtest, and how the codebase is put together — written to be followable without a finance background.

The short version

What it does. Every day, rank the S&P 500 on how strongly each stock has been rising, hold the top 10, size them so the jumpier names get less money, and go entirely to cash whenever the index itself falls below its 200-day average.

How it did over 28.7 years (Oct 1997 – Jun 2026), against buy-and-hold SPY, with no survivorship bias in the stock universe:

Strategy (top 10)SPY
CAGR8.69%9.27%
Sharpe0.670.55
Worst drawdown−38.1%−55.2%

The verdict in one sentence. It is built to lose less in a crash, not to win more in a rally — it beat the benchmark's risk-adjusted return while sitting in cash a quarter of the time, but trailed its raw return, and it lags badly in sharp recoveries.

The biggest caveat. Turnover runs ~11x a year and no trading costs are modeled. That omission could still erase part of the edge; see Limitations before quoting any number here.

The most useful thing measurement found. The strategy used to fire a rebalance the moment the top-N ranking changed — which sounds obviously correct — but that was cutting positions after a median of 5 days, before momentum could pay out. Replacing it with a monthly-only schedule (what's reported throughout this document) raised CAGR 2.1pp and cut turnover 3x (slide 11).

Where to go next. The thesis is why momentum should work at all; §1 is the full decision procedure, formula by formula; §2 is the evidence, including which market conditions actually favour it; §3 is the code; §4's Q&A works through the hard questions, including the ones this project answers badly.

Terms used in this guide, in plain English

  • Benchmark — the "did nothing clever" comparison. Here it's the S&P 500 (via the SPY ETF): what you'd have earned just buying and holding the market.
  • Backtest — running a strategy against historical prices to see what would have happened, without risking real money.
  • CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) — the one steady annual return that would have turned your starting money into your ending money. The standard way to compare returns over different time spans.
  • Volatility — how much a price bounces around day to day. Higher volatility = a bumpier ride, not necessarily a worse destination.
  • Sharpe ratio — return earned per unit of bumpiness. Two strategies can end up with the same return, but the one with the higher Sharpe ratio got there more smoothly. A Sharpe of 1.0 is solid; under 0 means you'd have done better in cash.
  • Sortino ratio — like Sharpe, but only counts downward bumpiness as risk. A strategy whose volatility is mostly big up-days scores better on Sortino than on Sharpe.
  • Drawdown — the biggest drop from a peak to the following low point before recovering. "−55%" means that at the worst moment you were down 55% from your previous high.
  • Survivorship bias — testing a strategy only on the companies that survived to today, which flatters results by quietly deleting the failures from history. See slide 2.
  • Rebalancing — periodically adjusting which stocks are held and how much money sits in each.
  • Turnover — how much trading a strategy does, as "how many times a year the whole portfolio gets replaced."
  • Risk parity / inverse-volatility sizing — instead of splitting money evenly, give shakier stocks a smaller slice and steadier stocks a bigger one.
  • Overfitting — tuning a strategy's settings until it looks great on past data, in a way that says nothing about the future — like memorizing last year's exam answers instead of learning the subject.

The investment thesis — why buying recent winners should work at all

Everything below this point is mechanics. This is the part that has to be true first, because a momentum strategy is a bet on a specific, well-documented, and genuinely strange claim: that past price movement predicts future price movement, which a strictly efficient market should not allow.

1. The empirical claim

Stocks that outperformed over the past 3–12 months keep outperforming, on average, over the next 3–12 months. Jegadeesh & Titman documented this in 1993 on US equities; it has since been found across decades, in most developed and emerging equity markets, and in bonds, currencies, and commodities. It is one of the few anomalies that survived being published — which is itself the most interesting fact about it.

2. Why it might exist — behavioral

  • Underreaction to news. Information diffuses gradually. Prices adjust to genuinely new information over weeks, not instantly, so the first part of a move predicts the rest. This is the specific effect the FIP score is designed to isolate.
  • The disposition effect. Investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long, creating artificial selling pressure on rising stocks that delays their full re-pricing.
  • Herding and delayed overreaction. Later buyers chase established trends, extending them past fair value — which is also why momentum eventually reverses, and why it can reverse violently.

3. Why it might exist — risk-based

The competing explanation is that momentum isn't a free lunch at all: it's compensation for bearing a real, nasty risk. Momentum portfolios are implicitly long high-beta, recently-rewarded, crowded positions, which is exactly the exposure that gets destroyed in a sharp regime reversal. On this reading the returns are earned, not mispriced — you are paid for holding something that occasionally crashes hard. Both explanations predict the same average return; they differ on whether it can be diversified away. It cannot.

4. Why it hasn't been arbitraged away

Momentum requires continuous, high turnover to maintain, so trading costs eat a large share of the paper return — which is precisely why this document treats unmodeled transaction costs as its most serious caveat. It also demands holding whatever is currently working, including at moments that feel obviously wrong, and it suffers multi-year stretches of underperformance that most capital will not sit through. The edge is guarded by cost and by discomfort, not by secrecy.

5. The known failure mode this design is built around: momentum crashes Momentum's characteristic disaster is not a slow bleed — it is a sudden, violent reversal at a market bottom, when beaten-down names rip upward and the recent winners the strategy holds are precisely the wrong book to own. This happened in 2009 and again in 2020. It is the reason this implementation is not a pure momentum strategy: the market regime filter exists specifically to force the portfolio to cash before a crash-prone reversal can happen, accepting a permanent, known cost — it will always re-enter late and give up most of a V-shaped recovery. That trade is visible, quantified, and unflattering in the market-condition breakdown: the strategy's entire edge is earned in downturns and handed back in sharp recoveries. Understanding this one trade-off is understanding the whole strategy.

01
Foundations

The core idea

Rotate into a small book of trending winners, and go to cash when the market itself is trending down. Three ideas from the momentum literature, combined — nothing invented from scratch.

Cross-sectional

Rank stocks against each other every period. Only the strongest relative performers (top 10 by default) are held — the classic Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) momentum effect.

Time-series

Each holding must also be trending up in absolute terms (price above its own 200-day average). Relative and absolute trend must agree — Antonacci's "dual momentum."

Regime-aware

A market-level trend filter can flatten the entire book to cash. The strategy doesn't try to stock-pick its way through a bear market.

Implements: the overall shape of MomentumStrategy.next() in strategy.py.

Foundations

The universe

What's eligible to be ranked in the first place — and why getting this right matters more than any formula on the later slides.

Three interchangeable sources, set by universe.source:

SourceWhat it resolves toSurvivorship bias
staticAn inline list in the config — configs/default.yaml ships ~140 tickers spanning mega-caps, sector SPDRs, factor/thematic ETFs, international/EM, and bonds/commodities, so momentum can rotate into whatever asset class is working.Whatever you put in it
index:sp500Scrapes today's S&P 500 constituent table from Wikipedia at run time.Yes — today's list applied to the past
index:sp500_point_in_timeReconstructs the index's actual membership month by month, from a stored table of monthly constituent snapshots (1996 → 2026). Each rebalance can only pick from the names genuinely in the index on that date.No
Why the point-in-time source exists Backtesting momentum against today's S&P 500 hands the strategy a roster picked with hindsight. Names that joined the index recently — precisely because they went up a lot — are retroactively made available to buy years before they qualified, while companies that were in the index and then failed out of it silently disappear from the test. For a strategy whose entire job is buying recent winners, that is not a small distortion. The point-in-time source removes it: on any given rebalance date the candidate list is the membership that actually existed then, including the names that later got acquired, delisted, or dropped. All results in §2 use this source.

Price history for every ticker that has ever been a member is loaded up front (including long-dead names), and each stock only becomes scoreable once it has enough of its own real trading history to fill every lookback window.

03
Step 1 · Every bar, before anything else

Regime filter — is the market itself trending up?

A single portfolio-level switch, checked before any stock is ever looked at.

Closebench(t) < SMA200[Closebench](t) ⟹ close every position, hold cash
  • Closebench(t) — the benchmark's (default SPY) closing price on day t
  • SMA200[·] — 200-day simple moving average (regime_ma_period)

When the benchmark is below its own 200-day average, every open position is closed and the strategy sits in 100% cash until the benchmark recovers back above it — no exceptions, regardless of how well any individual stock is scoring. This is time-series momentum applied at the market level: the mechanism that keeps the strategy out of prolonged drawdowns instead of fighting them stock-by-stock.

The filter is deliberately slow, and that is the whole trade A 200-day average cannot identify a top or a bottom — it can only confirm one well after the fact. So this filter is guaranteed to exit partway into a decline and re-enter partway into a recovery, every single time. That lag is not a flaw to be tuned away; it is the price paid for the protection, and it is exactly why the strategy loses far less than the benchmark in the 2008 and 2020 crashes while badly trailing it in the recoveries that follow (§2). A faster filter would cut the lag and buy more whipsaw; a slower one, the reverse.

Implements: MomentumStrategy.next(), the self.market.close[0] < self.regime_ma[0] branch in strategy.py.

04
Step 2 · Per stock, only when the regime is bullish

Stock-level trend filter

A cheap absolute-trend gate applied before any relative scoring happens.

Closei(t) > SMA200[Closei](t) ? eligible : excluded

A stock trading below its own 200-day average (ts_mom_lookback) is excluded outright, no matter how it would otherwise score — a stock can be the top cross-sectional scorer and still never make the book if it isn't itself in an uptrend. Combined with the momentum-must-be-positive check on the next slide, a stock actually has to clear two independent absolute-trend gates before its relative score is even considered.

Implements: the d.close[0] <= self._trend_sma(d): continue guard in strategy.py.

05
Step 3a · Scoring — signal 1 of 3

Momentum score

Mean percentage return across three horizons — smooths out any single lookback's noise.

Mi(t) = 13 ΣL∈{60,120,252} Closei(t) − Closei(t−L) Closei(t−L)
  • Mi(t) — momentum score for stock i on day t, as a fraction (0.10 = +10%)
  • L — one of the configured lookbacks, in trading days (~3 mo / ~6 mo / ~1 yr)

Averaging three horizons (lookbacks: [60, 120, 252]) is the intermediate-term momentum window from Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), blended across horizons so no single window's noise dominates the ranking.

Percentage return, not dollar change Each horizon is normalized by its own starting price, so the score is scale-free: a $15 stock that gained $3 (+20%) outranks a $500 stock that gained $40 (+8%). Ranking on raw dollar moves instead would systematically favour high-priced names for no economic reason. See slide 12 for this on a concrete three-stock ranking.

Implements: scoring.momentum_blend(), called from MomentumStrategy._momentum_score().

06
Step 3b · Scoring — signal 2 of 3

Frog-in-the-Pan (FIP) score

Rewards steady, "boring" grinding uptrends over the same total return achieved via a few violent jumps.

FIPi(t) = 1252 Σd=1 1[ ri(t−d) > 0 ]
  • ri(t−d) — stock i's daily return, d days back
  • 1[·] — indicator function: 1 if the condition holds, else 0
  • window: trailing 252 trading days (fip_lookback)

Simply the fraction of the last year's trading days that closed up. The academic basis (Da, Gurun & Warachka, 2014) is investor underreaction: information that arrives gradually, in a steady drip of small positive days, gets under-priced and keeps continuing — while the same total return delivered via a few large jump days tends to reflect one-off news that doesn't continue as reliably.

Implements: scoring.fip_score(), called from MomentumStrategy._fip_score().

07
Step 3c · Scoring — signal 3 of 3

Skewness score

The third standardized moment of the daily log-return distribution — how lopsided the recent moves have been.

Skewi(t) = (1/N) Σ (rᵢ − μᵢ)³ σᵢ³
  • ri — daily log returns over the trailing window; μi, σi — their mean and standard deviation
  • window: trailing 90 trading days (skewness_lookback)
  • positive skew = occasional large up-days on a base of small down-days ("lottery-like"); negative skew = the reverse
Read the sign carefully: this is a tilt, not a penalty The weight is named skewness_penalty, but it enters the combined score (next slide) with a positive sign and a positive default (0.5) — so as shipped, more positively-skewed, jumpier names score higher. To genuinely penalize jump-driven moves, as momentum-crash literature would argue for, set this coefficient negative in the config. The name describes an intent; the sign describes the behavior, and the sign is what runs.

Implements: scoring.skewness_score() (via scipy.stats.skew), called from MomentumStrategy._skewness_score().

08
Step 3d · Combining the three signals

Combined score

A simple weighted sum — no normalization between the three terms.

Scorei = wm·Mi + wf·FIPi + ws·Skewi
  • wm, wf, ws — momentum_weight, fip_weight, skewness_penalty; default 0.5 each
The three terms are summed raw, not rescaled — know their typical ranges Mi is a percentage return, typically in roughly [−0.5, 0.5]; FIPi is a fraction, strictly [0, 1]; Skewi typically runs about [−3, 3]. Because they're added without normalization, equal 0.5 weights do not mean equal influence — skew's wider natural range gives it more sway over the ranking than its weight suggests. Worth accounting for before reading the weights as a statement of intent.

Stocks are ranked by this score, highest to lowest — the ranking that feeds directly into top-N selection on the next slide.

09
Step 4 · Choosing the book

Selecting the portfolio

Four gates before a score is even computed, then a simple top-N cut.

Eligiblei(t) = [i ∈ Members(t)] ∧ [Closei > SMA200,i] ∧ [Mi > 0] ∧ [historyi ≥ min required]
  • Members(t) — the index constituents on day t (slide 2); with a point-in-time universe this is the membership that genuinely existed then
  • SMA200,i — the stock's own 200-day average (slide 4)
  • Mi — its momentum score (slide 5); must be strictly positive
  • min required — the longest lookback any signal needs, so no score is ever computed from partial data

A name must clear all four to be scored at all — a single failure is disqualifying regardless of how strong the rest look. Survivors are sorted by Scorei descending, and the top top_n (default 10) become the target portfolio for this bar. Everything currently held that isn't in that list gets sold on the next rebalance (slide 11).

Implements: the scoring loop and scores.sort(...)[: self.p.top_n] in MomentumStrategy.next().

10
Step 5 · Sizing the book

Position sizing — inverse volatility

Lower-risk names in the top-N get proportionally larger allocations — a simple risk-parity tilt.

wi = 1 / DDi Σj∈TopN 1/DDj , DDi = √ (1/N) Σ min(rᵢ, 0)²
  • wi — portfolio weight allocated to stock i
  • DDi — stock i's downside deviation: standard deviation computed only over its negative daily returns, trailing 126 days (vol_lookback) — a positive day contributes 0, not a penalty
  • a near-zero DD (e.g. no down days at all) is floored at a small constant rather than treated as "no data" — otherwise a genuinely low-risk stock would be excluded from sizing entirely instead of rewarded for it
Why downside deviation rather than plain volatility Ordinary standard deviation treats a violent move up as exactly as risky as a violent move down, which over-penalizes precisely the stocks a momentum strategy wants to hold. Downside deviation counts only the negative days — the same reasoning behind the Sortino ratio — so a stock that is "volatile" because it keeps gapping up is not punished for it.

An alternative is available as sizing_method: cap_weighted, which multiplies the numerator by an approximate market cap (shares_outstanding × close), tilting toward larger, more liquid names at equal risk. It is not the default; inverse_vol is.

Every rebalance also reserves 1% of portfolio value as a buffer — order_target_value sizes against today's close but fills at the next bar's open, and a position sized at exactly 100% of value can get margin-rejected on a single bar of price drift.

Implements: scoring.downside_deviation() and scoring.inverse_vol_weights(), called from MomentumStrategy.next() via _volatility().

When does any of this actually trade?

Rebalance mechanics

The rule governing when the portfolio trades has changed once already, based on measurement — this slide documents both versions and why the second replaced the first.

What's shipped today, in one line The strategy trades only on a calendar schedule (rebalance_frequency: monthly or quarterly) — full stop. Every number in §2 is produced by this design. It replaced an earlier design, described first below, that also rebalanced immediately on any change to the top-N ranking; that design is no longer in the codebase, but the comparison between the two is the most important measurement in this document, so it's kept in full.

The prior design — two triggers, whichever fired first

A
Membership changeAny name entered or exited the top-N versus the last rebalance → rebalance fired immediately, regardless of schedule.
B
Calendar triggerThe configured rebalance_frequency elapsed — fired even if the top-N membership hadn't changed at all.

Trigger B was added because inverse-vol weights (previous slide) go stale between membership changes — without it, the same names could sit at their original sizing for months while their relative volatility drifts, with nothing otherwise forcing a resize. That reasoning was sound; Trigger A is the part that turned out to be a mistake, for the reasons below.

On a rebalance (both designs): positions no longer in the new top-N are closed first; inverse-vol weights are recomputed for the new top-N; each target position is opened/resized via order_target_value.

Finding 1 — the calendar cadence was almost irrelevant to the prior design

Before anything else changed, switching rebalance_frequency from monthly to quarterly under the prior design moved CAGR, Sharpe, and turnover by less than 0.1 percentage points in either direction, at both top_n settings. Counting the actual rebalance events explained why: Trigger A fired 66x/year at top_n=5 and 101x/year at top_n=10 — a median gap of 2–3 days — so in well over 98% of rebalances Trigger A had already fired long before the calendar window could elapse. Changing the calendar cadence changed almost nothing, because the calendar was almost never the thing deciding when to trade. That naturally raised the next question.

Finding 2 — removing Trigger A was a large improvement, which is why it's gone

If Trigger A was effectively the only trigger, what happens if it's removed and trading is capped to the calendar schedule alone? Same universe, same signals, same window — only the trade-timing rule differs:

Rebalance ruleCAGRSharpeMax DDTurnover
n=5 · prior design (A + B)9.50%0.63−46.7%33.5x/yr
n=5 · shipped: calendar-only, monthly11.61%0.75−46.0%11.0x/yr
n=5 · calendar-only, quarterly (rejected)7.85%0.57−47.7%6.4x/yr
n=10 · prior design (A + B)8.43%0.64−43.5%31.4x/yr

Calendar-only monthly improved CAGR by 2.1pp and Sharpe by 0.12 at n=5, cut max drawdown by 5.4pp at n=10, and cut turnover roughly 3x in both — which is why it's what's shipped today. Calendar-only quarterly was worse than the prior design on every metric and was rejected. That the two calendar-only variants disagreed so sharply is itself informative: the gain is not simply "trade less." A quarterly schedule trades even less and performed worst of all. The schedule has to be slow enough to stop reacting to noise, yet still fast enough to keep the momentum signal fresh — monthly happens to sit near that balance, and a quarter is demonstrably too slow.

Why it helped — Trigger A was forcing exits on noise, not signal

No commissions, spread, or slippage are modeled anywhere in this backtest, so this gap cannot be a trading-cost effect. It has to come from which trades happen and how long they are held. Instrumenting every completed position under both designs said exactly that: under the prior design the median holding period was 5 days, with 79.5% of all positions closed inside 21 days; under calendar-only monthly the median is 32 days, with only 26.6% closed inside 21 days. Grouping every completed trade by how long it was held (n=5) shows why that is decisive:

Holding periodPrior design: share of tradesPrior design: mean returnShipped: shareShipped: mean return

The pattern is the same under both designs and is the entire story: every trade closed within 21 days loses money on average, and essentially all of the strategy's profit comes from positions held 22 days or longer. Momentum needs weeks to pay out. The prior design's Trigger A fired on any rank change — including the constant, meaningless shuffling of names hovering around the top-N cutoff — so it kept selling positions within days of buying them, on noise rather than on any real trend reversal, and 79.5% of its trades never survived into the range where the edge actually lives. Calendar-only monthly does not pick better stocks; it picks the same stocks and simply stops interrupting them, moving 73.4% of trades into the profitable 22-day-plus buckets versus the prior design's 20.5%.

Status, stated plainly Calendar-only monthly is merged and is what every number in this document reflects, including §2's headline table, growth chart, and regime breakdown. Quarterly is supported in config but not used by default, given the result above. Trigger A was removed outright rather than kept as a config toggle, since keeping it would preserve the exact behavior this measurement argues against.

Implements: MomentumStrategy._is_rebalance_due() and the rebalance gate in next().

What this is (and isn't)

A research/backtesting tool, not a trading system: no live or paper-trading execution, no walk-forward optimization or parameter search tooling, no multi-strategy portfolio composition — one strategy, one portfolio.

Beating (top_n=5) or nearly matching (top_n=10) a fully-invested benchmark's return while sitting out a quarter of the period is where the drawdown and volatility advantages come from. It is also the main source of risk in the other direction: the filter is a 200-day moving average, so it exits well after a top and re-enters well after a bottom.

Shaded bands mark four widely-recognized downturns (dot-com bust, financial crisis, COVID crash, 2022 bear market) referenced in the market-condition breakdown below. Note the log scale: equal vertical distances are equal percentage moves, which is what makes a 28-year chart readable at all — on a linear axis the 1990s would be an invisible flat line.

What to look for:

  • In the 2008-09 band the strategy lines go almost flat while grey falls by more than half — that flatness is the portfolio sitting in cash, and it is the single clearest picture of the regime filter working. The protection is far weaker in the 2000-02 band, where top_n=5 still lost nearly 44% (a momentum-led selloff hits momentum holdings before the index-level filter ever triggers).
  • Straight after each band, grey climbs faster than blue and orange. That is the cost of the same mechanism: re-entry only happens once the 200-day average confirms the recovery, by which point a chunk of the rebound is gone.
  • Both lines' lead over grey peaks right after a crisis and erodes across the long bull runs that follow — that shape repeats throughout. But the two settings end up in very different places by 2026: blue's lead over grey narrows but never closes (it is still roughly 1.8x grey's value at the end, having peaked near 2.6x around 2016), while orange's does close — grey overtakes it during the 2009-2020 climb and stays ahead. That's the same number as the headline table's top_n=10 cumulative return trailing SPY's.

Risk profile

The benchmark's worst drawdown is the 2007-10 → 2009-03 financial crisis, −55.2% peak-to-trough. Neither strategy configuration avoids that period, but both cut it materially (−46.0% and −38.1%). Their own worst drawdowns land in the dot-com unwind instead — which is the expected failure mode for momentum: a sharp rotation out of the exact names that had been leading, happening before the index-level regime filter has had time to react.

Turnover of ~11x a year is the figure worth quoting — a rebalance now fires only on the calendar schedule (slide 11), not on every change in top-N membership. That rule was measured and replaced specifically because the membership-triggered version churned at ~30x a year while cutting most positions before they had time to pay off; the version measured here is already the improved one. These results still model no commissions, no spread, and no slippage, so turnover remains the right thing to interrogate before trusting any number here — at 11x a year the realistic cost drag is far smaller than it would have been at 30x, but it isn't zero. See Limitations.

Which market conditions actually favor this strategy

The single Sharpe number in the headline table averages over nine very different market regimes. Splitting the same verified return series into them (dot-com bust through the 2023-2026 AI-led bull run) shows the average is hiding a sharp, consistent pattern rather than a uniform edge:

Four of the four labeled downturns — dot-com, GFC, COVID crash, 2022 — show the strategy losing meaningfully less than buy-and-hold, most dramatically in the GFC (down only 4.1%/2.3% versus the benchmark's −54.8% over that window) and the COVID crash (down 10.5%/11.9% versus −33.4%, saved in just 33 days). This is the regime filter and defensive rotation doing exactly what they're designed to do: get out of the way of a broad market decline rather than trying to stock-pick through it.

The cost shows up just as clearly on the other side. The single worst relative period in the whole 28 years is the COVID recovery (2020-03 to 2022-01): the benchmark more than doubled (+114%) while both configurations returned less than half of that (+44%/+48%). This is the textbook failure mode of trend-following — a 200-day moving average cannot confirm a new uptrend until a lot of the recovery has already happened, so a V-shaped rebound is close to the worst-case scenario for this design. The long, grinding 2009-2020 bull market shows the same pattern at a larger scale (+254–330% versus the benchmark's +514%, a 183-to-259-point gap over 10.9 years): a decade of low-volatility, broad-market appreciation with few sharp drawdowns to avoid gives the regime filter little to add, while the residual turnover (unmodeled cost, see Limitations) keeps working against it.

The pattern in one sentence This strategy is built to lose less in a crash, not to win more in a rally — the 28-year Sharpe advantage comes almost entirely from the four downturn rows above, and is given back, not gained, during long bull markets and especially sharp V-shaped recoveries.

How these were produced

  • Universe: index:sp500_point_in_time — actual month-by-month S&P 500 membership, so no survivorship or look-ahead bias in the candidate list (slide 2).
  • Data: daily OHLCV via yfinance, cached in cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet. Price history is loaded for every ticker that was ever a member, ~779 of which have retrievable data. Prices are auto-adjusted for both splits and dividends, so strategy and benchmark are compared on a consistent total-return basis.
  • Benchmark: SPY, measured over the strategy's own date range.
  • Starting capital: $100,000. Orders are market orders filled at the next bar's open.
  • Everything else: stock defaults — lookbacks: [60, 120, 252], vol_lookback: 126, skewness_lookback: 90, fip_lookback: 252, ts_mom_lookback: 200, regime_ma_period: 200, weights 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.5, rebalance_frequency: monthly, sizing_method: inverse_vol.

Reproduce either run directly:

uv run momentum run configs/sp500_pit_1997_2026_n5.yaml  --output tearsheet_n5.html
uv run momentum run configs/sp500_pit_1997_2026_n10.yaml --output tearsheet_n10.html

Each writes a full quantstats tearsheet with a Summary Statistics block on top. Expect roughly 10–15 minutes per run: ~700 price feeds across ~7,200 trading days is genuinely a lot of per-bar computation, and almost none of the wall time is data fetching once the cache is warm.

Determinism was verified, not assumed A backtest is only evidence if the same inputs give the same answer every time. Both configurations were run twice, independently, and their daily return series diffed against each other: identical to the last decimal. Every number in this section comes from those verified runs. This matters more than it sounds — the price cache is populated incrementally from a live data vendor, so "same config, same result" is a property that has to be actively maintained and checked, not one you get for free.

The full monthly return series behind the table and chart above — top_n=5, top_n=10, and SPY, compounded from the same verified daily series — is saved at results/monthly_returns_1997_2026.csv.

Limitations — read before quoting any number above

1. No transaction costs, spread, or slippage are modeled Turnover is now ~11x a year, down from ~30x under the design that produced results before the rebalance-rule change (slide 11), so the omission is meaningfully less consequential than it was — but a few basis points per trade still compounds into a real drag at 11x over 28 years, and this remains unmodeled.
2. Constituent history is reconstructed, not vendor-supplied Monthly membership comes from a community-maintained reconstruction of the index, not an authoritative point-in-time vendor feed. It is dramatically better than applying today's list to the past, but it is not audited data, and the pre-2001 portion is the least reliable stretch.
3. Roughly a third of historical constituents have no retrievable price data Companies that were delisted, acquired, or went bankrupt long ago frequently return nothing from Yahoo Finance. Those names are skipped, which means the deepest failures in the index's history are under-represented in the candidate pool — a residual bias pointing in the optimistic direction.
4. Two settings on one history is not a validated strategy These are two runs over a single (long) period. Nothing here has been walk-forward validated or tested out-of-sample on another index or market, and no parameter on slides 5–10 has been swept. Treat the table as a measurement of this implementation, not as evidence about momentum investing generally.
5. Cash earns nothing while the regime filter is out of the market For roughly 27% of the period the book sits in cash at a 0% risk-free rate. Over 1997–2026 — which includes stretches of 5%+ short rates — a real implementation parking that cash in T-bills would have earned meaningfully more than these figures credit it with. This one biases the results pessimistically, unlike the others on this list.

3Code Structure & How to Run It

The package was refactored from a single 300+ line script (raw/main.py, kept for reference) into a proper src-layout Python package with the trading math separated from the backtrader execution engine, so the math can be unit tested without spinning up a backtest.

universe.py Resolves a ticker list from config Three sources: static (inline list), index:sp500 (live Wikipedia scrape of today's members), and index:sp500_point_in_time, which returns every ticker ever in the index plus a month-end → member-set lookup so the strategy can restrict each rebalance to the membership that actually existed then (slide 2).
data.py Per-ticker OHLCV fetch via yfinance, backed by a git-tracked parquet store sharded across four files (cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet); also caches shares outstanding (cache_data/shares_outstanding.parquet) for cap-weighted sizing Long-format store (ticker, Date, OHLCV columns) split across 4 shards keyed by crc32(ticker) % 4, loaded once per process. Coverage-based, not time-based: a ticker's rows are reused only if their date range already covers the request; otherwise it downloads, merges, and rewrites only that ticker's shard. Checked into git specifically so a fresh clone has working data for any ticker/range someone has already fetched, with no network call. Tickers Yahoo has no data for at all (long-delisted names, dead symbols) are recorded in cache_data/no_data_tickers.parquet so they aren't re-attempted every run — with ~1,200 historical constituents, ~429 of which are permanently unavailable, that alone saves minutes per run. Shares outstanding is a single current snapshot per ticker (via fast_info), used as shares × close(t) to approximate historical market cap — cheap, but technically uses information not available on past rebalance dates.
backtest.py Orchestration: builds Cerebro, adds benchmark as datas[0] and universe as datas[1:], runs, and converts the strategy's recorded daily portfolio value into a returns series Missing per-ticker data is logged and skipped, not fatal; missing benchmark data is fatal. Validates every feed has enough trading days for the configured lookbacks (_min_required_bars) before handing it to backtrader. Also aligns every ticker onto the benchmark's own trading-day calendar (_align_to_calendar) so all feeds are the same length regardless of listing date, and passes each ticker's real listing position to the strategy so a stock only becomes eligible once it has genuinely accumulated enough of its own history.
reporting.py Generates the quantstats tearsheet, then injects a custom Summary Statistics block into the same HTML file Backtest period, absolute/relative total return, CAGR (strategy vs. benchmark), Sharpe ratio, and annualized turnover — turnover isn't something quantstats can compute (it only sees returns, not trades), so it's tracked separately in strategy.py. Replaced a ~230-line hand-rolled Excel reporting pipeline in the original prototype.
cli.py argparse-based entrypoint: momentum run <config> [--output path] Registered as a console script (project.scripts in pyproject.toml), so it runs as momentum once installed.

4Design Notes & Defending the Work

Three things: the decisions that aren't obvious from reading the code, the behaviors most likely to surprise someone modifying it, and a worked set of the questions this project invites — including the ones it doesn't answer well. If you're about to change something, read the first two. If you're about to discuss this project, read the last one, and read it alongside §2 Limitations, since almost every hard question traces back to a limitation that's already documented there.

Key design decisions

  • Scoring math is framework-agnostic. scoring.py has zero backtrader imports specifically so the strategy's core logic can be unit tested in milliseconds, without a full backtest run.
  • Rebalance trigger — went from dual, to measured, to calendar-only. The original prototype only rebalanced on membership change, which left inverse-vol weights stale for names that stayed in the top-N for months, so a calendar trigger was added alongside it. Instrumenting the result later showed the membership trigger fired 66–101x a year and preempted the calendar one in over 98% of rebalances — and that removing it outright raised CAGR by 2.1pp while cutting turnover 3x, so it was removed. The current strategy trades only on the calendar schedule. The lesson generalizes: a rule that sounded obviously right ("react as soon as the ranking changes") was quietly the single most expensive decision in the strategy, and only measurement surfaced it. See slide 11.
  • Cache is git-tracked, sharded, and coverage-based rather than time-based. data.py keeps every ticker's OHLCV history in cache_data/prices_00..03.parquet (long format: ticker, Date, OHLCV), checked into the repo so a fresh clone has working data for anything already fetched — no network call needed. A ticker's rows are reused only when their date range already covers what's requested, not on a TTL, so re-running the same backtest twice does zero network calls the second time either way. Rows are assigned to a shard by crc32(ticker) so a run that touches a few tickers rewrites only the shards those tickers live in, rather than the whole dataset — which keeps each commit's diff proportional to what actually changed, and keeps every file well under hosting size limits. Prices are stored as float32 with zstd compression; the discarded precision is far below any price's real significance.
  • Config validated at load time. Both missing required fields and an invalid rebalance_frequency raise immediately in config.py, rather than surfacing as a confusing failure deep inside a multi-minute backtest run.
  • Per-ticker data gaps degrade gracefully; benchmark gaps don't. A missing/empty ticker is logged and skipped — the backtest proceeds with the rest of the universe. A missing benchmark is a hard failure, because the regime filter has no fallback.
  • Turnover has to be tracked at the trade level, not derived from returns. Every other headline stat (CAGR, Sharpe, total return) is a pure function of the returns series and comes straight from quantstats. Turnover needs actual traded dollar value, which only exists inside backtrader's order lifecycle — hence MomentumStrategy.notify_order() accumulating it, threaded back through run_backtest()'s return value into reporting.py.
  • Feed length is validated up front, then normalized. _min_required_bars rejects any feed too short to ever satisfy the configured lookbacks — a clear error for the benchmark, a logged skip for a ticker. Everything that survives is then reindexed onto the benchmark's trading-day calendar so all feeds share one clock, with each ticker's real listing date tracked separately to gate eligibility. Uniform feed lengths keep one stock's readiness from interacting with another's.
  • Per-stock signals are computed inline, not registered as indicators. Momentum, FIP, skewness, volatility, and the trend SMA are all computed on demand inside next() from raw price windows. Registering hundreds of per-stock indicator objects instead would couple every stock's warmup to every other stock's, which is a poor fit for a universe whose members list changes over decades.
  • The benchmark is measured over the strategy's own window. The strategy has no returns until its regime SMA warms up; comparing it against a benchmark measured from an earlier start date would silently hand the benchmark months of free return. reporting.py clips the benchmark to the strategy's date range before computing any comparison stat.

Gotchas worth knowing cold

1. skewness_penalty currently rewards, not penalizes, skew See slide 7 — the coefficient is added with a positive sign and a positive default weight.
2. The three score terms are summed without normalization Equal 0.5 weights do not mean equal influence — skew's natural range is several times wider than momentum's, so it moves the ranking more than its weight implies. See slide 8, and slide 12 for a concrete ranking flip caused by a 0.20 difference in skew.
3. Ranking and sizing deliberately disagree The top-ranked name routinely receives the smallest allocation, because sizing is driven purely by downside deviation and ignores the score entirely (slide 10). That's intended, not a bug — but it surprises people reading a position list for the first time.
4. Sizing uses a 99%-of-value buffer Because order_target_value sizes against today's close but fills at the next bar's open, a position sized at exactly 100% of portfolio value can get margin-rejected on a single bar of price drift. The 1% reserve is a deliberate, documented workaround, not an arbitrary constant.
5. rebalance_frequency accepts only null, "monthly", or "quarterly", and the choice now matters a lot config.py enforces this at load time; strategy.py's own _is_rebalance_due would otherwise raise a less-clear ValueError deep inside a run for any other string. This is the opposite of a historical version of this codebase, where the choice barely moved the numbers because a membership-change trigger dominated it — that trigger is gone, this is now the only thing that decides when the portfolio trades, and monthly beats quarterly by a wide, measured margin. See slide 11.
6. The date range must cover at least ~253 trading days Driven by fip_lookback + 1; a shorter range raises a clear ValueError (benchmark) or skips the ticker (per-name) instead of an opaque crash — see the note under §3 Running with S&P 500.
7. Cash earns 0% while the regime filter is out of the market There is no risk-free rate credited to idle cash, and the strategy sits in cash roughly a quarter of the time (§2). Every reported return is understated by whatever that cash would have earned in T-bills.

Questions you might get, and how to answer them

Grouped by what the question is really testing. The uncomfortable ones are included deliberately — the weaknesses in §2 Limitations and Gotchas are all fair game, and the strongest answer to each is a direct one.

The strategy and why it should work

Why should buying recent winners work? Isn't that the opposite of buying low?

It is, and that's the point — momentum and value are two of the best-documented anomalies and they point in opposite directions on the same stock. The behavioral case is that information diffuses gradually, so prices underreact and the first part of a move predicts the rest; the disposition effect (selling winners early) and herding both reinforce it. The risk-based case is that momentum portfolios are crowded, high-beta, recently-rewarded positions that periodically crash, so the return is compensation rather than a mispricing. Both stories predict the same average return. I don't need to resolve which is right to trade it, but the second one tells me the drawdowns are structural and can't be diversified away. See the investment thesis.

If it's this well documented, why hasn't it been arbitraged away?

Because capturing it is expensive and uncomfortable. It requires continuous high turnover, so trading costs consume a large share of the paper return — which is exactly why unmodeled transaction costs are the biggest caveat on my own results. It also requires holding whatever is currently working at moments that feel obviously wrong, and sitting through multi-year stretches of underperformance. The edge is protected by cost and by career risk, not by obscurity.

Your momentum lookback runs right up to the current day. Standard academic momentum skips the most recent month — why don't you?

That's a genuine deviation and a fair criticism. The standard academic construction skips the most recent month — Carhart's UMD factor and most subsequent work use "12-2" momentum, measuring from 12 months ago to one month ago — specifically because short-horizon returns exhibit reversal rather than continuation. The last month's move tends to partially undo itself, so including it mixes a known-negative signal into the score. momentum_blend() measures each lookback straight to the latest close with no skip, so this implementation carries that contamination. It's the first change I'd test on the scoring side, and it's cheap: one index offset in one function.

Why three lookback horizons instead of one?

Any single lookback is a somewhat arbitrary choice that gets noisy at its own boundary — a stock can look strong on a 60-day window and weak on a 252-day one purely because of where a single large move happens to fall. Averaging 60/120/252 keeps the ranking from hinging on one window's accident. It's a variance-reduction choice, not a return-enhancing one, and it also reduces the number of free parameters I could accidentally overfit.

Holding only 5–10 names out of 500 is very concentrated. Isn't that reckless?

It's deliberate, and the results show the trade-off directly. top_n=5 earns a much higher return (11.61% vs 8.69% CAGR) with a somewhat worse drawdown (−46.0% vs −38.1%) and higher volatility; top_n=10 gives up return for a meaningfully smoother ride. Both beat the benchmark on Sharpe, which is the more durable result, but at 5 names the return edge is now large enough to win outright, not just on a risk-adjusted basis. Concentration is how a cross-sectional momentum signal expresses conviction — diluting to 50 names would push the portfolio toward the index and the signal toward noise. The honest caveat is that at 5 names, single-stock idiosyncratic risk is a real and largely unrewarded exposure.

What's the difference between the two momentum concepts you're using?

Cross-sectional momentum ranks stocks against each other and holds the relative winners — that's the top-N selection. Time-series momentum asks whether an asset is rising in absolute terms, against its own history. This design requires both to agree: a stock must be a relative winner and above its own 200-day average, and the whole book goes to cash when the index itself fails the same absolute test. Requiring agreement is what stops the strategy from dutifully buying "the best-performing stocks in a collapsing market," which pure cross-sectional ranking would happily do.

Design and implementation choices

How do you know you don't have look-ahead bias?

Three separate places it could enter, each addressed. Universe: membership comes from month-by-month point-in-time snapshots, so a rebalance can only choose names actually in the index on that date (slide 2) — the alternative, scraping today's constituent list, silently hands the strategy every future index addition. Signals: every score is computed from a trailing window ending at the current bar, and a stock isn't scoreable until it has genuinely accumulated enough of its own history. Execution: orders are placed on the signal bar but filled at the next bar's open, never at the close I made the decision on. The one honest exception is shares outstanding for cap-weighted sizing, which is a current snapshot applied to past dates — it's off by default for that reason.

Why separate scoring.py from strategy.py?

Testability and reuse — the same momentum/FIP/skewness math could back a different execution engine or a research notebook without dragging in backtrader. In practice the payoff was speed of iteration: the scoring math is unit-tested in milliseconds, so I never had to run a 12-minute backtest to find out whether a formula was right.

Why inverse-volatility sizing instead of equal weight?

It's a simple risk-parity tilt: among the top-N momentum names, it reduces the influence of the single most volatile holding on portfolio swings, without needing a full covariance-based optimizer. Note it uses downside deviation rather than plain standard deviation, because ordinary volatility treats a violent up-move as identically risky to a violent down-move — which systematically penalizes exactly the stocks a momentum strategy wants to own. The deliberate consequence is that ranking and sizing disagree: the top-ranked name routinely gets the smallest allocation.

Why hold cash instead of shorting or rotating into bonds in a bear market?

Simplicity, and because the regime filter is a blunt instrument. A 200-day moving average is late by construction — it exits after a decline is well underway and re-enters after a recovery is well underway. Layering shorts on top of a signal with that much lag adds a way to lose money in exactly the whipsaw conditions the filter handles worst. Rotating into bonds is more defensible and is already possible via the static universe, which can include TLT/GLD and let momentum choose between asset classes directly. It's also worth noting the backtest credits idle cash 0% — a real implementation holding T-bills for the ~27% of the time it's out of the market would have earned meaningfully more than these numbers show.

Walk me through a decision you made that the data later proved wrong.

The rebalance trigger. The design fired a rebalance immediately whenever the top-N ranking changed, which sounds obviously correct — react as soon as your signal changes. Instrumenting it showed that trigger fired 66–101 times a year and preempted the calendar schedule in over 98% of rebalances, giving a median holding period of 5 days. Bucketing every completed trade by holding length showed why that was expensive: every trade closed within 21 days had negative average return, and essentially all the profit came from positions held 22+ days. The rule was reacting to meaningless shuffling around the top-N cutoff and cutting positions before the signal could pay out. Removing it raised CAGR 2.1pp and cut turnover 3x (slide 11). The general lesson is that the most expensive assumption was the one that felt too obvious to test.

Reading the results

Your top_n=10 CAGR is 8.69% against SPY's 9.27%. Why is a strategy that underperforms interesting?

Because it earned that return while invested only 73% of the time, with 13.8% volatility against the benchmark's 19.5% and a −38.1% worst drawdown against −55.2%. That's a higher Sharpe (0.67 vs 0.55) and higher Sortino. The claim isn't "this beats the market" — for top_n=10 it doesn't, on raw CAGR, though it's close. The claim is that it delivers comparable or better return per unit of risk while spending a quarter of the period in cash, which is a different and more defensible product: it's a risk-managed equity exposure, not an alpha engine. top_n=5 happens to beat the benchmark outright on every axis at once (§2), which shows the risk-managed framing isn't a consolation prize dressed up as a virtue — it can also just win.

In which market conditions does this actually work, and where does it fail?

The pattern is sharp and consistent. It loses far less in downturns — during the GFC the strategy lost only 4%/2% against the benchmark's 55%, and in the COVID crash 11%/12% against 33%. It lags in rallies, and lags badly in sharp V-shaped recoveries: during the 2020–2022 COVID recovery the benchmark more than doubled (+114%) while the strategy returned well under half of that. The whole 28-year Sharpe advantage is earned in four downturn windows and given back across long bull markets. That's not a flaw to fix; it's the direct arithmetic consequence of a 200-day filter that can only confirm a reversal well after it starts. See the market-condition breakdown.

How do you know this isn't overfit?

Mostly, I don't — and I'd rather say that than oversell it. The honest position is that these are two parameter settings run once over one long history, with no walk-forward validation, no out-of-sample test on another index or market, and no parameter sweep (Limitation 4). What I can say is that the parameters are literature defaults rather than values I searched for — 200-day regime filter, 60/120/252 lookbacks, 252-day FIP — so there was little opportunity to fit them to this sample, and the risk-adjusted result holds across both top_n settings rather than at one lucky value. Proper validation is the work this needs next, and until it's done these numbers measure this implementation on this history, not momentum investing generally.

If you had to name the single biggest risk in the reported results, what would it be?

Transaction costs. Turnover runs around 11x a year and nothing in the backtest models commissions, spread, or slippage (§2 Limitations). That's already a 3x improvement over where this project started — turnover was ~30x a year until the rebalance rule was rebuilt specifically because it was overtrading (slide 11) — but it isn't zero, and the returns as computed are not large enough to assume they survive realistic frictions on top. Measuring the actual cost impact at 11x turnover is the single most valuable next piece of work on this codebase.

Are there biases left in the data that flatter these results?

Yes, and they point in both directions. Flattering: roughly a third of historical constituents have no retrievable price data — mostly companies delisted or bankrupted long ago — so the index's worst failures are under-represented in the candidate pool, and the membership reconstruction is community-maintained rather than a vendor-audited feed, with the pre-2001 stretch least reliable. Pessimistic: cash earns 0% for the ~27% of the period spent out of the market, when a real implementation would have held T-bills through years of 5%+ short rates. I don't think these cancel neatly, and I wouldn't claim they do.

Weaknesses and what you'd do next

What's the biggest methodology caveat in the strategy logic itself?

skewness_penalty rewards rather than penalizes skew under the shipped defaults (slide 7) — it's added with a positive sign and a positive weight, so the strategy tilts toward jumpy, lottery-like names. That's the opposite of what the parameter name implies and the opposite of what momentum-crash literature argues for. Compounding it, the three score terms are summed without normalization, and skew's natural range (roughly ±3) is several times wider than momentum's (roughly ±0.5), so despite equal 0.5 weights, skew moves the ranking more than either of the other two signals. The worked example on slide 12 shows a 0.20 difference in skew overturning a 3.2-point difference in momentum.

What happens in a sideways, choppy market?

This is the design's weakest regime and the filter doesn't help. A 200-day average only reacts to the benchmark's trend, so a market that chops sideways without a sustained decline never triggers the cash switch — the strategy stays invested and rotates through whichever names are transiently leading. Under the baseline rebalance rule that's actively costly, since it churns on rank noise with a 5-day median holding period. It's the same weakness the rebalance finding addresses.

Given unlimited time, what would you do next, in order?

Three things, in order. One: model transaction costs — it's the largest remaining unknown and it determines how much of the current edge is real. It's more urgent now than before, not less: the rebalance-rule change already cut turnover 3x and improved returns (slide 11), so the strategy can now absorb more realistic cost assumptions than it used to, which makes this the right moment to actually measure it. Two: fix the two scoring issues — normalize the three score terms so the weights mean what they say, and skip the most recent month in the momentum lookback to stop feeding short-term reversal into the signal. Three: walk-forward validation and an out-of-sample test on a different index, because until that exists this is a well-measured backtest rather than evidence about the future.

What would make you abandon this strategy?

Concretely: if modeled transaction costs at realistic spreads erase the Sharpe advantage over buy-and-hold, the strategy has no reason to exist in this form. Beyond that, the structural risk is that the regime filter's protection is what the entire edge rests on — it's earned in four downturn windows out of nine regimes. If that mechanism stopped working, either because drawdowns arrive faster than a 200-day average can react (2020 was already close) or because the market stops producing the kind of sustained declines it's designed to sidestep, then what's left is a concentrated, high-turnover portfolio that lags in bull markets, which is a strictly worse product than an index fund.
